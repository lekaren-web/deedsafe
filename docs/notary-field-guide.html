Start here

The short version

  1. A notary verifies people, not documents. They confirm who signed and that the person signed willingly. They don't check whether the document is true, legal, or fair.
  2. Most deed theft is identity fraud. An impostor poses as the owner and signs a brand-new deed. Hashing that fake deed doesn't make it less fake. DeedSafe doesn't stop impostors.
  3. DeedSafe's real strength is the notary's alibi. If a notary records every real notarization, a deed carrying their seal with no matching record is a red flag. That targets the cases where fraudsters fake a notary or steal a real notary's seal.
  4. Focus on real estate, but sell to two sides. Loan signing agents create the records; title companies lose money to fraud and have budgets, so they're the likelier buyers. Notaries in general earn too little per act for $49/month to make sense.
  5. Never call it notarization. DeedSafe timestamps and fingerprints. Avoid "digital notary," "unforgeable," "court-proof," and "zero liability." Those claims create legal risk instead of removing it.

Basics

What a notary actually does

A notary public is a state-commissioned official who acts as an impartial witness. Every state sets its own rules, but the core job is the same: make sure the right person is signing, and that they're doing it knowingly and willingly.

The main notarial acts

ActWhat the notary certifiesTypical use
AcknowledgmentThe signer was identified and declared they signed willingly. The signer can sign before meeting the notary, but must appear in person to acknowledge it.1Deeds, mortgages, powers of attorney. This is the one that matters for real estate.
JuratThe signer swore or affirmed that the document's contents are true, and signed in front of the notary.1Affidavits, sworn statements
Oath / affirmationThe person made a spoken promise to tell the truth.Depositions, officials taking office
Copy certificationA copy matches the original (only in some states, and only for some documents).2Non-public records

The kinds of notary you'll meet

TypeWho they areRelevance to DeedSafe
Traditional notaryAnyone commissioned by their state. Nearly 4.4 million in the US, many notarizing only occasionally at a bank or office.3Huge group, low spending
Loan signing agent (LSA)A notary trained to guide borrowers through mortgage and refinance paperwork. Paid per package, roughly $50–$200.4Your best notary-side user. Handles deeds every week.
Electronic notary (IPEN)Notarizes digital documents with an e-signature and e-seal, with the signer still physically present.Documents are already digital, so hashing fits naturally
Remote online notary (RON)Notarizes over live video with ID checks and a recording. Permanent laws in roughly 45–49 states plus DC, depending on how you count.5Usually works inside a platform (Proof, NotaryCam, etc.) that already keeps tamper-evident records
How a real estate signing goes

A title company hires an LSA. The LSA meets the seller, checks their government ID, has them sign the deed, completes the acknowledgment certificate, applies their seal, records the act in their journal, and returns the package. The title company then sends the deed to the county recorder, who scans it into public records. That last step turns the original into a scanned image, so the original file's hash won't match the county's copy.

Boundaries

What notaries can't do, and why it matters to you

Unless they're also a licensed attorney, a notary can't practice law. In practice that means no legal advice, no explaining what a document means, no helping fill in or choose a legal form, and no picking the certificate type for the signer.26 Notaries also can't notarize a transaction they have a financial interest in, and many states ban non-attorney notaries from calling themselves "notario."7

Why this matters for DeedSafe
  • Don't build features that tell a notary which certificate to use or what a document means. That could lead your users into the unauthorized practice of law.
  • Your ID-check checkboxes should record what the notary attests to. They should never advise on the law.
  • DeedSafe isn't a notary, so marketing must never suggest it performs notarizations or gives notarizations legal effect.

Closest existing concept

The notary journal

Many states require notaries to keep a journal: a log of every notarization with the date, signer, act type, document type, and how the signer was identified. It's the closest thing in the notary world to what DeedSafe does. Electronic journals face rules that sound a lot like your product's promises:

  • Can't be changed. Entries can't be edited after they're made.8
  • Notary in control. Most states require the journal to be under the notary's sole control, password-protected (ideally with two-factor login), and encrypted.8
  • Kept for years. Illinois requires at least 7 years after the last electronic act; North Carolina requires a backup for 10 years.910
  • Sensitive data limits. Texas, for example, bars recording driver's license numbers, Social Security numbers, or biometrics in the journal.11
  • Sometimes public. In some states, journal entries can be requested as public records.11
Positioning tip

Present DeedSafe as a companion to the journal ("an independent, public timestamp for each notarization"), not as a replacement. Being someone's official journal means meeting every state's journal rules, which is a much bigger compliance job.

The problem you're solving

How deed fraud actually happens

The most common pattern is seller impersonation. A fraudster finds a property with no mortgage and an owner who isn't around, poses as the owner, and "sells" it.

Picks a target: vacant land, absentee owner, no mortgage, or a recently deceased owner12
Contacts a real estate agent by email or text, avoids meeting, and asks to sign remotely
Gets the deed "notarized" with a fake ID, a fake notary, or a stolen real notary seal
Title company closes and the money is wired out
County records the deed; the real owner finds out months later

Most important section

What DeedSafe can and can't stop

A hash proves this exact file existed at this time and hasn't changed since. It says nothing about who signed it or whether the signer was who they claimed to be. Here's how that plays out:

ScenarioDoes DeedSafe help?Why
An impostor shows a fake ID to a real, honest notaryNoThe deed is new and genuinely notarized. Hashing it only proves the fake deed exists.
A fraudster forges a notary's seal and signature on a deed the notary never sawYesThe alibi case. If the notary records every real act, a title company can check that notary's records and find nothing for that date. Works only if the notary uses DeedSafe consistently and the title company actually checks.
A fully fake notary with an invented commissionPartly"This notary isn't on DeedSafe" is a weak signal while few notaries use it. State commission lookups do more here.
Someone alters pages or numbers after signingYesRe-hash the original digital file: any change, even one byte, breaks the match.
Checking the county's scanned copy years laterPartlyThe scan never matches the original hash. Someone has to get the original PDF from the title company, verify it, then compare the two copies by eye.
Owner wants to know if someone recorded a deed on their homeNoThat's recorder alert services (free in many counties) and title monitoring.
What this means for the product
  • Tie records to the notary. Every stamp should be findable by notary and date, not just by hash, so a title company can ask "did this notary really notarize something on June 3?"
  • Keep personal data off the blockchain. Entries are public and permanent. Put only the hash and a random record ID on-chain; keep names and addresses out.
  • Pitch it accurately: "proves which documents a notary actually handled, and when" is true and valuable. "Prevents deed fraud" is too broad.

Recommendation

Focus on real estate. Treat it as a two-sided network:

  • Loan signing agents stamp each notarization. Keep this cheap or free, since every notary who joins makes the network more useful. Their pitch: "If your seal is ever forged, you can prove which signings you actually did."
  • Title and escrow companies pay to verify notaries before closing, and to require their signing agents to use DeedSafe. They feel the losses, they already buy fraud tools (CertifID, for example, sells seller identity checks18), and they can push adoption onto their signing agents.
Selling to title companies brings duties

Title companies are financial institutions under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA). The FTC's Safeguards Rule makes them vet and monitor their software vendors.19 Expect security questionnaires asking about encryption, two-factor login, incident response, and eventually a SOC 2 audit. In 2025, the FTC acted against a mortgage company that sent customer files to a vendor it hadn't vetted properly.19 Your choice not to store documents helps a lot here, but document names and user records still count.

Context

The legal landscape

States are acting on deed theft

  • About 12 states now have dedicated deed-theft laws, and 29 have none, according to one industry scorecard (counts vary by source).20
  • Arizona now requires notaries to take a thumbprint for deeds, made recording a fraudulent document a felony, and requires photo ID to record a deed in person.21
  • California requires every county to notify owners within 30 days of a deed being recorded, starting January 1, 2027.21
  • Texas created the crimes of real property theft and fraud; Virginia gave settlement agents an identity-verification duty; Alabama requires seller ID checks in higher-risk deals.21

Most of these laws alert owners after a fraudulent deed is recorded. Tools that act before closing are the gap, and that's DeedSafe's opening.

Blockchain records as evidence

  • Federal Rules of Evidence 902(13) and 902(14) let electronic records and copies verified by hash be admitted without a live witness, but only with a written certification from a qualified person and notice to the other side.22 The record doesn't prove itself; someone has to certify it. I found no federal case applying these rules to blockchain records.
  • Vermont (12 V.S.A. § 1913) explicitly makes blockchain records self-authenticating, but only with a sworn declaration from a qualified person.23
  • Arizona (A.R.S. § 44-7061) says blockchain signatures and records can't be denied legal effect just for being electronic. That's about contracts, not courtroom evidence.24
What this means

Be ready to provide a certification service: someone at DeedSafe who can sign a declaration explaining how a record was created. That's a real feature title companies and lawyers would value. "Automatically court-admissible" is a claim you can't make.

Also on title companies' minds

FinCEN's residential real estate reporting rule took effect March 1, 2026. A federal court in Texas struck it down on March 19, 2026, and FinCEN appealed in May. Title companies currently don't have to file those reports, but the rules may change.25

Staying legal: your checklist

Words matter

Avoid

  • "Digital notary," "notarize," "notarization"
  • "Unforgeable," "foolproof," "tamper-proof"
  • "Court-admissible," "self-authenticating"
  • "Lawsuit immunity," "zero liability"
  • "Verifies identity," "prevents deed fraud"
  • "Shifts 100% of liability to the notary"

Use instead

  • "Timestamp," "fingerprint," "anchor"
  • "Tamper-evident record"
  • "Independently verifiable on a public ledger"
  • "Supports your records with an independent timestamp"
  • "Helps detect altered documents and forged notary seals"
  • "Records the steps the notary attested to"

Before you launch

Bring this list

Questions for a lawyer

Look for a lawyer who works with real estate tech or fintech startups. Bring these:

  1. Which states should I launch in first, given their notary and deed-fraud laws?
  2. How should my Terms of Service limit liability, given that I sell to notaries and title companies?
  3. Do the attestation checkboxes help or hurt me if a notary is fooled and someone sues?
  4. Can notaries use DeedSafe alongside their official journal without breaking journal or confidentiality rules in their state?
  5. What GLBA and state privacy obligations do I take on when selling to title companies?
  6. Who at DeedSafe can serve as a "qualified person" for evidence certifications, and what should that declaration say?
  7. How do I structure commission-only sales reps legally in the states where they'll work?
  8. Is "DeedSafe" safe to trademark, and is any of my marketing language misleading?

Reference

Glossary

Acknowledgment
A notarial act confirming a signer's identity and that they signed willingly. Standard for deeds.
ALTA
American Land Title Association, the title industry's trade group. Publishes the "Best Practices" title companies follow.
Commission
A notary's state-issued license, valid for a set term.
County recorder
The local office that records deeds and makes them public. Also called the register of deeds or clerk.
E&O insurance
Errors and omissions insurance. Covers a notary's mistakes; often required by title companies.
GLBA
Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act. Federal law making financial institutions, title companies included, protect customer data and vet their vendors.
Hash (SHA-256)
A 64-character fingerprint of a file. Any change to the file produces a completely different hash.
Jurat
A notarial act where the signer swears the document's contents are true.
Loan signing agent
A notary who handles mortgage and real estate document packages.
Notary journal
The notary's log of every act. Required in many states, with rules on retention and access.
RON
Remote online notarization, done over live video with ID checks and a recording.
Seller impersonation
Fraud where someone poses as a property owner to sell or mortgage it.
Title company
Researches ownership history, insures the title, and often runs the closing and holds the money.
Unauthorized practice of law
Giving legal advice or legal services without a law license. Notaries must avoid it, and so should software built for them.
